\section{Discussion and Future Work}

\noindent\textbf{Architecture Meaning of the Frontier:}
\SystemName does not prove current quantum advantage. It turns advantage into a computer-architecture boundary: once task, quality target, and native baseline are fixed, the remaining gap can be assigned to logical execution, shot parallelism, decoder/control overhead, native-baseline strength, or quantum algorithm quality. The important output is not one speedup slogan; it is a workload-specific target for what a future QPU must change before the full quantum-circuit application beats the native HPC path.

\noindent\textbf{Future QPU Control Stack:}
The main implication is that a single ``faster quantum computer'' roadmap is too coarse. Surface-code demonstrations emphasize real-time decoding and cycle time~\cite{googleqec2025belowthreshold}, LDPC proposals trade qubit overhead against connectivity and decoder requirements~\cite{bravyi2024qldpcmemory}, and FTQC roadmaps include modularity, magic-state resources, and control hardware~\cite{ibmftqcroadmap2025}. Our workload results map those device choices to applications. ML and Opt. need quality-preserving encodings, better trainable/search structure, batched submission, and low-latency hybrid feedback. Chem exposes repeated energy evaluation, so decoder bandwidth, controller scheduling, measurement aggregation, observable batching, and queue latency become first-order targets. Sim. points toward Hamiltonian-simulation-oriented analog/digital execution, where runtime advantage must be checked against fidelity, Trotter order, and observable quality. Annealing hardware may help optimization cases that embed cleanly as energy minimization~\cite{dwaveannealingdocs}; it is not a general replacement for the gate-model paths measured here.

\noindent\textbf{HPC--Quantum Integrated Architecture:}
QHPC systems should treat a QPU as an accelerator inside a scientific computing workflow, not as a detached queue. \SystemName supplies the quantitative contract such systems need: native deadlines, quality tolerances, circuit-evaluation counts, shot-parallel regions, and serial decoder/control floors. With those signals, an integrated runtime can co-schedule CPU/GPU kernels, simulator backends, QPU batches, measurements, classical optimizers, and quality checks as one workflow.

\noindent\textbf{Scope and Future Work:}
The largest modeling risk is an underpowered native baseline. We reduce it by selecting the fastest valid method among ML, optimization, molecular-energy, and Hamiltonian-simulation solvers, then measuring the strong-native shift. Stronger natives on larger tasks, including deeper CNN/ResNet families, boosted trees, tuned MILP/metaheuristics, tensor networks, projector QMC, and CCSD(T)-class references, can still move the frontier rightward; the method quantifies how much. \cuQuantum is also instrumentation, not the future hardware stack. Hardware-specific studies should replace $T_{\mathrm{error}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{shots}}$ with code distance, cycle time, logical-error budget, magic-state throughput, decoder latency, controller bandwidth, and device concurrency across superconducting, trapped-ion, photonic, or analog/digital assumptions. Future versions should attach energy and mitigation cost by comparing $E_{\mathrm{native}}=N_{\mathrm{GPU}}P_{\mathrm{GPU}}T_{\mathrm{native}}$ with refrigerator, decoder, control, host, and error-mitigation overhead over $T_{\mathrm{qhw}}$. Bundled Slurm runs, 12 warmup-separated trials, zero failures, maximum quantum-runtime CV is 0.0400, and artifact audits reduce timing and provenance risk.
